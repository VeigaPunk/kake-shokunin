### The 3 most important points

1. **Coverage moves past the HTTP cache into state the browser keeps.** The new note names its own frontier: rounds 1–100 built deep HTTP caching and validator tools, but "a fresh HTTP response is not the only state a returning user can see". It covers three things the earlier curation corpus didn't (the note says it screened 412 local curation files): service-worker controller generations, back/forward-cache restored documents, and `srcset`/`sizes` resource choice. The key lesson for the service-worker part is that `skipWaiting()` and `clients.claim()` can leave a document loaded under worker A fetching assets from worker B. So "the new worker is active" isn't an acceptance criterion; the document, controller and asset combination is.
2. **A concrete rule for when sources disagree.** The broad web.dev bfcache guide still says Chrome's `Cache-Control: no-store` change is "underway". The narrower, dated Chrome page documents the 2025 rollout and its safeguards (eviction on cookie or auth changes, shorter timeout). The note picks the dated product page and still limits it to Chrome, not all browsers. It also warns against dropping privacy-related cache directives just to raise a performance score. That's good calibration: newer and narrower wins, and the scope stays labelled.
3. **Each proposed exercise ships with a negative control and a stopping rule.** All three exercises are explicitly "not run", but each one names the wrong checker it must defeat: success judged from `registration.active` alone, `pagehide.persisted=true` treated as proof of a later restore, and reading the fallback `src` instead of `currentSrc`. Each is also bounded (6, 6 and at most 32 cases; loopback-only origin; temporary profile; no third-party images). The note ends with a stopping rule: stop adding cases when two in a row only repeat a known mechanism, and consolidate before deciding whether training is justified. That's the clearest template so far for how a reading candidate could become a tool.
