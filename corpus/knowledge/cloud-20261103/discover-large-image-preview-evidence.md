# What a large-image preview can tell you about Discover performance

Google's Discover feed can show an article with a large image, but permission to use a large preview is not a promise that the article will appear, that a particular image will be chosen, or that traffic will rise. A historical publisher case helps illustrate both the opportunity and the limits of the evidence.

## The publisher reports

In an [August 2021 case study](https://developers.google.com/search/case-studies/large-images-case-study), Google reported that food blog Kirbie's Cravings saw its click-through rate (CTR) increase 79% after adding a setting allowing large image previews. The publisher also described increased click-through from Discover and site traffic. The case does not provide the starting and ending CTRs, impression and click counts, precise comparison windows or a controlled counterfactual. The reported change is therefore not evidence that the setting alone caused the increase, nor a forecast for another site.

The same case reports a 30% increase in CTR for the magazine Istoé and a separate 332% increase in clicks over six months. A 332% increase corresponds to 4.32 times the baseline, not 3.32 times. Google attaches the six-month period to clicks; the case does not provide enough detail to assume the CTR comparison used the same windows or independently reproduce either calculation. Content, audience, seasonality and other changes might have contributed. These are historical reports, not a statement about either publisher's present configuration or performance.

## What the current guidance says

According to Google's [Discover documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/google-discover), content indexed by Google and meeting Discover content policies is automatically eligible without special tags or structured data. Eligibility does not guarantee that it will appear.

Google recommends relevant, high-quality images, particularly large ones, for Discover. Its current recommendations include a width of at least 1,200 pixels, more than 300,000 total pixels, a 16:9 aspect ratio, and large previews enabled through the `max-image-preview:large` setting or AMP. These are recommendations, not universal conditions for Discover eligibility. Google may crop images automatically, so a landscape crop should retain important details. Relevant `og:image` or schema.org image markup can influence the thumbnail selected, but cannot force a choice. Google advises against generic logos or text-heavy images for that purpose.

The [robots meta specification](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/robots-meta-tag#max-image-preview) describes `max-image-preview` as a maximum preview-size setting. `none` requests no image preview, `standard` permits a default-sized preview, and `large` permits a larger one up to the viewport width. Without the setting, Google may show a default-sized preview. The rule can be conveyed in page metadata or an HTTP response header and applies across several Google surfaces, including Discover. It does not force crawling, indexing, inclusion, a particular card or image, or a gain in CTR or visits. AMP is an alternative in Google's guidance, not a requirement or a guarantee.

Google says the more restrictive rule applies when robots instructions conflict. Its specification also describes exceptions for separately granted content permissions, including certain structured data and licensing arrangements. Consider all applicable rules and permissions, rather than reading one tag in isolation.

Crawl access, indexing, snippets, preview size and rights are separate questions. A robots.txt restriction can prevent Google from reading page-level instructions and does not reliably remove an indexed URL. A `noindex` instruction requests exclusion from Google Search, but the crawler must be able to access the resource and read it. Neither mechanism is private access control. Google's [noindex guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/block-indexing) explains the distinction. `nosnippet` suppresses text snippets and video previews, though a static thumbnail may still appear; `max-snippet` limits text rather than image previews. Google's [snippet guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/snippet) covers textual snippets separately. Allowing a preview does not establish ownership of a photograph, permission to license its use, or consent from people pictured.

For a meaningful comparison, record the relevant surface and population, definition of CTR, impressions and clicks, included URLs, dates and time zone, baseline and comparison windows, and concurrent changes to images, content, audiences or distribution. CTR is a rate: for a comparable population and period, it is commonly clicks divided by impressions. It is not itself the number of clicks or visits.

## Twelve hypothetical decisions

Each example below is invented as a manual reasoning exercise. It is not a test or a finding about a real website or account.

1. **Relative change versus percentage points.** A hypothetical CTR rises from 10% to 17.9%. That is a 79% relative increase and a 7.9 percentage-point increase, not a 79-point increase. These are illustrative rates, not Kirbie's actual baseline or result.
2. **CTR versus clicks.** Discover produces 100 clicks from 10,000 impressions in one hypothetical period and 80 clicks from 4,000 in another. CTR rises from 1% to 2%, a 100% relative increase, while clicks fall 20%. A higher rate does not establish more clicks or visits. Check that numerator, denominator, surface and population are comparable.
3. **Comparison windows.** A report assigns Istoé's six-month click window to its CTR change and calls 332% growth 3.32 times the baseline. Both claims go beyond or misread the case. The six-month qualifier is attached to clicks, and 332% growth gives 4.32 times the baseline. Request the original windows and counts.
4. **Eligibility versus display.** An indexed, policy-compliant article has no special tag. One editor says it cannot be eligible for Discover; another says it must appear. Both are wrong under Google's stated guidance: no special tag is required for eligibility, and eligibility does not promise display or a large preview.
5. **Image dimensions.** A proposed image changes from 1,200 × 250 to 1,280 × 720 pixels. The first meets the width recommendation but has exactly 300,000 pixels, not more than 300,000, and is not 16:9. The second has 921,600 pixels and 16:9 proportions. Record which image and crop are used; meeting recommendations does not guarantee selection or display.
6. **Conflicting limits.** A page's HTML permits a large preview but an applicable response header specifies `standard`. Flag the conflict. Google's general rule favors the more restrictive instruction, subject to its documented separately granted permission exceptions. Inspect the applicable rules and rights rather than assuming the HTML wins.
7. **Text versus image controls.** An editor adds `nosnippet` and assumes no static image can appear, or adds `max-snippet:20` and assumes it caps image size. Neither follows from Google's specification. A static thumbnail may remain with `nosnippet`, while `max-snippet` limits text rather than image or video previews. Check the separate image setting and permissions.
8. **Crawl access and noindex.** A page contains `noindex` and large-preview instructions, but robots.txt disallows Googlebot from crawling it. Do not claim Google read either rule or that the URL was removed. If exclusion is intended, an accessible `noindex` is a possible next step, followed by authorized verification. Robots restrictions are not authentication.
9. **Image metadata.** A proposed `og:image` points to a site logo although a relevant large article photo exists. The team assumes the metadata forces Google's choice. Consider identifying a relevant representative image in `og:image` or schema.org and reviewing crop and access. The markup can influence selection but does not establish the actual choice or prove a crawl occurred.
10. **Privacy and rights.** An article contains an identifiable person's sensitive photo licensed for display only on the publisher's site. An editor says enabling large previews grants all necessary rights and protects privacy. Pause pending a review of the license, consent and privacy implications. The setting establishes neither rights nor confidentiality.
11. **Policy and platform changes.** A hypothetical CTR changes after a tag edit while the site's story mix, season, Discover policies and Search systems also change. Preserve dated documentation, comparison windows and concurrent changes. Do not assign the result to the tag or assume historical guidance remains current. Google's Discover guidance notes that Search updates and other changes can affect Discover traffic.
12. **A prospective comparison.** A team proposes randomly assigning comparable eligible pages to large-preview permission or an unchanged control, with outcomes, windows and analysis specified in advance. This could support stronger causal inference than a simple before-and-after comparison if assignment, exposure, contamination, sample size and missing data are evaluated. Record impressions, clicks, actual preview exposure if measurable, uncertainty and population changes. Randomization cannot guarantee a result, and the historical case did not report such an experiment.
