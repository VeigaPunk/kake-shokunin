# What Saramin's search story can and cannot tell us

A search improvement report is useful evidence, but it is not automatically an experiment. Google's 2020 account of Saramin illustrates the difference between documenting a set of changes, describing what happened afterward, and proving what caused it.

The discussion of URL moves below is separate present-day guidance for hypothetical sites. Google's Saramin account does not say that Saramin migrated its site. The twelve examples are invented decisions, not observations of Saramin or another real property. No live site, search account, or search-result test was performed for this article.

## Read the results in context

According to Google's [Saramin case study](https://developers.google.com/search/case-studies/saramin-case-study), the Korean job platform registered its site in Search Console in 2015. During the first year, the team checked crawl errors and fixed issues to help Googlebot crawl the site for indexing. Google reports a **15% increase in organic traffic** in that period.

The team later removed meta tags filled with unhelpful keywords, used canonical URLs, removed duplicate content, and added applicable structured data, starting with JobPosting, Breadcrumb and Estimated salary. They also studied Google's documentation and used diagnostic tools. Google describes a gradual improvement in Search Console coverage and a later acceleration in traffic.

During the September 2019 peak hiring season, the case reports a **102% year-over-year increase in organic traffic from Google Search**. It also reports a **93% increase in new member sign-ups from Google Search organic traffic** and a **9% year-over-year increase in conversion rate from that traffic**. The displayed sign-up figure does not independently state a year-over-year comparator; it should not be supplied by assumption. These are relative increases, not percentage-point changes. As an illustration, a conversion rate rising from 10% to 10.9% increases by 9% relative to its starting rate, or by 0.9 percentage points. Those illustrative rates are not Saramin data.

The case provides no controlled comparison that isolates the effect of any individual change. The outcomes may reflect the combined work and other influences, including seasonality or concurrent changes; their separate contributions are not quantified. The report does not establish Saramin's current results or predict another site's outcomes. In particular, it does not prove that canonical tags, structured data, keyword-meta removal, or any other single action produced the reported increases.

## Making a URL decision today

A canonical URL expresses a preferred version among duplicate or very similar pages. Google's [canonical guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls) describes redirects and canonical link annotations as strong signals, and sitemap inclusion as a weaker signal. It recommends consistent signals, including internal links to the preferred URL and a self-referencing canonical on that page. Google can nevertheless select a different canonical. A site's declaration is not an observation of Google's choice, and a rewrite that serves content at the original address is not the same thing as a redirect.

If a site is actually changing URLs, Google's [site-move guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes) recommends building an old-to-new mapping, updating links and annotations, and using server-side permanent redirects such as 301 or 308 when possible. Send an old URL directly to a relevant final equivalent rather than through a long chain. A generic home page is not automatically a replacement for unrelated content: sending many unrelated pages there may confuse users and be treated as a soft 404. A genuinely consolidated page can be a suitable shared destination. Google's guidance says to retain redirects as long as possible, generally at least a year. Crawling and indexing proceed per URL, and search visibility can fluctuate during a move.

A deliberately retired page with no relevant replacement can return a real 404 or 410. Google's [HTTP status guidance](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/troubleshooting/http-status-codes) says Search removes previously indexed 4xx URLs over time; 429 is treated differently as an overload signal. An error page returning 200 can be treated as a soft 404. Conversely, a 404 for content that ought to exist deserves investigation rather than automatic acceptance.

Google's [URL Inspection help](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9012289) distinguishes the indexed report from the live test. The indexed report can show Google's selected canonical from the most recently indexed version, which may be older than the current page. A live test reports on current indexability but cannot predict which canonical Google will select. A live result may describe a destination reached through a redirect. The report may reveal neither that intervening redirect nor the address of the page actually examined. A positive reported result alone therefore does not prove that the submitted URL served the intended content, that it is indexed, or that it appears in search. Separately examine the redirect chain and final content when authorized.

For an investigation, keep a dated record for each URL: its source in the old inventory, intended disposition, proposed destination and equivalence rationale, observed response and redirect hops, final content, canonical declaration, sitemap and internal-link targets, and any indexed-view choice and crawl date. Mark unknowns rather than filling them in. Compare like metrics over defined periods and segments; Search Console clicks and analytics sessions, for example, are not interchangeable. Seek authorization before collecting data, running tests, or changing a site.

## Twelve hypothetical decisions

These examples are invented. Each conclusion is limited to the facts given; none reports a test or outcome on a real site.

1. **Incomplete inventory.** A proposed move has 900 old URLs in its sitemap, while logs and inbound-link records reveal 100 more. Combine and deduplicate the known sources into a dated inventory, note where each URL was found and what coverage remains unknown, and give each known URL a disposition. The sitemap alone does not prove completeness. ([Site-move guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes))
2. **Relevant replacement.** `/jobs/designer` is being retired permanently, and `/careers/design` contains the same role listings. A direct server-side 301 or 308 is a reasonable proposed mapping. Record why the content is equivalent; a written mapping is not proof of the live response. ([Site-move guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes))
3. **Irrelevant replacement.** An expired specialist job page has no equivalent, but the plan sends it to a generic home page. Reject that mapping as misleading and potentially a soft 404. Find an actually relevant replacement or use an appropriate gone response if retirement is intentional. A 301 alone does not establish equivalence. ([Site-move guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes))
4. **Intentional disappearance.** An event has ended, its content has no replacement, and a hypothetical response record shows 404. Record the removal and its rationale rather than inventing a redirect. Previously indexed URLs can be removed over time, not necessarily immediately. If authorized, verify the actual status rather than assuming an error message implies 404. ([HTTP status guidance](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/troubleshooting/http-status-codes))
5. **Unexpected disappearance.** The new URL mapped to a current job category hypothetically returns 404, although the category should still exist. Mark it as a defect to investigate, not an intentional retirement. The remedy depends on whether the content or a genuine equivalent exists. ([Site-move guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes))
6. **Aligned preferences.** A `?sort=default` URL and `/careers/design` contain substantially the same content. The proposed clean URL is self-canonical, links and sitemap favor it, and the duplicate points to it as canonical. Record these as aligned preferences, not a guarantee of Google's choice. If a redirect is proposed, distinguish its actual response from a rewrite. ([Canonical guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls))
7. **Conflicting preferences.** A duplicate declares `/careers/design` canonical, but the sitemap and internal links favor the parameter URL. Flag the conflict and propose aligning signals to the chosen equivalent. Sitemap inclusion is a weaker signal, not proof that Google selected that URL. ([Canonical guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls))
8. **Preference differs from selection.** A page declares A, while a dated hypothetical indexed report says Google selected B at its last crawl. Record both and the crawl date, then investigate content and signal differences. Neither the declaration nor a stale indexed report alone establishes the current state. ([URL Inspection help](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9012289))
9. **A live result has limits.** A hypothetical live-test report for a submitted new URL says the tested page is potentially indexable; no indexed report is available. Record the result and date, with indexing and Google's selected canonical unknown. The test might have followed a redirect without showing it or the final URL, so it does not prove the submitted address served the intended content. Examine the chain and final content separately if authorized. ([URL Inspection help](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9012289))
10. **Unlike traffic metrics.** A post-move report shows 1,000 fewer analytics sessions on old URLs and 900 more Search Console clicks on new ones. Do not subtract them or call the difference a loss: they are different measures. Obtain comparable sources, definitions, periods, segments and attribution rules, and consider measurement changes and seasonality before drawing a conclusion. This is an analytical application of Google's [monitoring guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes), not a Saramin result.
11. **A bundled change.** A hypothetical site changes canonicals, content and structured data together; its measured conversion rate rises from 10% to 10.9%. That is a 9% relative increase, or 0.9 percentage points, for the specified comparison. It does not prove structured data caused the change. Preserve the denominator, period, traffic source and concurrent changes; a credible controlled comparison could strengthen causal inference. The rates are invented. ([Saramin case study](https://developers.google.com/search/case-studies/saramin-case-study))
12. **Aggregate success, individual defect.** New-site traffic rises overall, but a hypothetical high-value old URL passes through two intermediate redirects to an unrelated page, and its indexed report is stale. Keep the aggregate trend separate from this URL's defect. Record each hop, the final destination and relevance, the indexed-view date and missing evidence; propose a direct relevant destination if one exists. The aggregate rise does not prove that this mapping worked or that Google currently selected the intended URL. ([Site-move guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes); [URL Inspection help](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9012289))
