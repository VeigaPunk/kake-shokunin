# Stale-while-revalidate: what a Google ad-script experiment showed

This historical case illustrates a caching trade-off, not a current diagnosis or configuration. The twelve examples are hypothetical manual reasoning exercises. No additional site, cache, experiment or model was tested in preparing this lesson. Applying the lesson to a system requires current evidence and authorization.

## A script, a cache policy, and an experiment

[Jonathon Imperiosi and Beng Eu's Google case study](https://web.dev/case-studies/ads-case-study-stale-while-revalidate), last updated **5 March 2020**, examines Google Publisher Tag (GPT), a Google Ad Manager script used for display ads. Its bootstrap, `gpt.js`, had a short 15-minute freshness lifetime intended to allow rapid updates and rollbacks; it loaded other implementation scripts with longer lifetimes. The authors describe the previous stale-copy path as a blocking network revalidation that delayed the first ad request. Their account is historical, not a claim about all caches or today's GPT.

In 2016 the team added `private`, `max-age=900`, and `stale-while-revalidate=3600` to the response's cache policy. The design lets a supporting cache use an eligible stale copy while attempting a nonblocking refresh, accepting a period in which older code may be used. The header alone does not show that a browser reused the copy, revalidated successfully, or received the latest script. Under [RFC 9111](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9111.html#section-5.2.2.7), unqualified `private` prevents a shared cache from storing the response, but does not by itself prevent storage by a private cache or guarantee confidentiality.

To evaluate the feature, the authors report comparing a temporarily disabled 1% of Chrome traffic with a sampled 1% where it was enabled. They call the disabled group **experimental** and the enabled group **control**. These labels are counterintuitive but are the article's own; do not swap them. Across a reported sample of **5.2 billion Google display ad impressions over two weeks** (impressions, not users), the enabled control group observed increases of **0.3% in ad impressions, 0.5% in revenue, 2% in early ad-script loads, and 1.1% in successful ad-script loads overall**. Early meant less than 500 milliseconds from page-load start. The article credits its chart to Google internal data from June to July 2019 and attributes the outcome to more successful loads, mostly early.

This is the authors' comparison, not a prediction for another publisher, browser, script or period. The four reported increases are percentages; the article’s chart separately uses a percentage-point change axis, and these are not interchangeable. Do not add the reported figures together. The article does not provide enough detail to reconstruct assignment, exact per-group sample counts and denominators for each metric, uncertainty intervals, or other changes and possible biases. Retain the population, time window, group labels, metric definitions and direction, and seek the missing design and uncertainty details before making stronger causal or economic claims.

## Reconciling the stale window with the source's wording

The source describes stale reuse and a background refresh for requests arriving 15–60 minutes after the cached version was last obtained. Separately, [RFC 5861 section 3.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5861.html#section-3.1) describes its `stale-while-revalidate` interval as **additional** to the freshness lifetime. For the stated values, its illustrative reading is a 900-second fresh lifetime followed by up to 3,600 seconds of allowed staleness: approximately **15 to 75 minutes of HTTP current age**. That is a difference in source wording, not a license to rewrite the article's claim as if it said 75 minutes or to assert what actually happened in every Chrome request. [RFC 5861](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5861.html) is an **Informational** RFC, not a standards-track specification; the later [RFC 9111](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9111.html) is the standards-track HTTP caching specification. It postdates the 2019 experiment and supplies general current caching context, not proof of the experiment's implementation.

HTTP current age is not simply elapsed wall-clock time since a browser received a response. It can include age accumulated before receipt, response transit and residence time, and uses Date, Age, and timing information as applicable. Consult [RFC 9111's age calculation](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9111.html#section-4.2.3). A matching cached response can be fresh when current age is less than its freshness lifetime; at 900 seconds it is no longer fresh under that comparison. For the worked model below, assume the only applicable freshness lifetime is 900 seconds, the stored representation matches the request, and the stale extension is supported and not overridden by other directives or constraints. At 4,500 seconds, the 3,600-second staleness allowance is at its outer boundary; do not promise a stale hit at that exact instant. Beyond it, the RFC 5861 extension alone no longer justifies continuing stale reuse. Other directives, request conditions, cache policy, eviction, network state and implementation details can change the actual outcome.

RFC 5861 says a cache **may** serve stale under the extension and **should attempt** asynchronous revalidation when it does. Permission is not a guarantee of a cache hit, background request, successful validation, or new content. Revalidation needs an eligible request during the window; time passing without requests does not itself trigger the example's asynchronous validation. A validator may allow conditional revalidation, but the particular status and result depend on the response and server. A failed background request is not proof that a newer representation reached the cache; `stale-while-revalidate` is distinct from `stale-if-error` and does not itself promise unlimited offline use. See [RFC 5861 sections 3 and 4](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5861.html#section-3) and [RFC 9111 on stale responses](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9111.html#section-4.2.4).

## A different cache problem: personalized HTML

Separating visitor-specific data from a reusable HTML shell addresses which representations may be stored or shared safely. The GPT case addresses a different question: whether a private cache may use an older bootstrap script while attempting to refresh it in the background. Both depend on cache boundaries, but `private` and a visitor-independent shell are not the same architectural claim. Neither the header nor a generic shell establishes that a system protects personal data; inspect the complete response, cache key, authorization and actual behavior.

For a future authorized investigation, record the URL and representation variant, date and time, HTTP status, relevant cache headers and calculated current age, browser/cache and version, whether the entry existed, request conditions, and observable validation outcome. Preserve measurement populations, group labels, definitions, time windows and uncertainty separately from any header snapshot. Do not record cookies, credentials, or personal response bodies; use synthetic or appropriately redacted evidence. A single header or timing cannot establish all paths or prove causation.

## Twelve hypothetical manual cases

These are independent invented inputs and expected judgments, not observations or test results. In cases 1–4 assume the worked model above, a matching stored response, no overriding directive, and a cache that supports the extension.

1. **Before freshness expiry.** A request finds current age 899 seconds and freshness lifetime 900 seconds. **Expected decision:** classify it as fresh and eligible for ordinary reuse under the assumptions; the stale extension is not needed. Do not promise a hit if the entry is missing or a different request constraint applies. [RFC 9111 freshness](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9111.html#section-4.2).
2. **At freshness expiry.** A matching stored response has current age exactly 900 seconds. **Expected decision:** it is no longer fresh under the RFC 9111 age-versus-lifetime comparison; in this model it is at the start of potential stale reuse. A supporting cache may use it and should attempt background validation if serving it stale, but need not do so. [RFC 9111](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9111.html#section-4.2); [RFC 5861](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5861.html#section-3).
3. **Within the additional window.** Current age is 3,900 seconds, so the response is stale by 3,000 seconds. **Expected decision:** the RFC 5861 model permits, but does not guarantee, stale reuse with an attempt at nonblocking validation. This is 65 minutes of HTTP age, outside the article's stated 15-to-60-minute wording but inside the illustrative 15-to-75-minute arithmetic. Do not claim it is an observed GPT result.
4. **Outer edge and beyond.** One request sees age exactly 4,500 seconds, another 4,501 seconds, with no successful revalidation. **Expected decision:** 4,500 is the outer boundary of the modeled allowance, so do not promise an exact-edge stale hit; at 4,501 the 3,600 seconds of staleness have passed and the extension alone should no longer justify serving the response stale. The next handling depends on other applicable rules, including any separate stale permission. [RFC 5861](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5861.html#section-3).
5. **Age already accumulated.** A browser receives a response with a valid Age value of 600 seconds; 400 seconds of residence pass before the next request, and no revalidation occurs. **Expected decision:** do not call the response 400 seconds old merely because that is time in this browser. Its current age includes prior age and applicable timing, so it is at least around 1,000 seconds under this simplified input and is stale relative to a 900-second lifetime. Compute precisely from the full timing and headers before a real decision. [RFC 9111 age](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9111.html#section-4.2.3).
6. **Extension unsupported.** A cache has a matching stale response and receives the stated directives but does not implement `stale-while-revalidate`. **Expected decision:** the directive's presence does not establish asynchronous stale reuse. Apply the cache's supported rules and other applicable directives; absent another permission, the stale response needs validation or other normal handling before reuse. Do not claim that the header automatically enables support. [RFC 9111 extensions](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9111.html#section-5.2.3).
7. **Private versus shared.** An unqualified `private` response is offered to a shared CDN cache and a browser's private cache. **Expected decision:** the shared cache must not store it under that directive; the private cache may store it subject to other rules. Do not infer that either has stored or served it, or that `private` guarantees secrecy. [RFC 9111 private](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9111.html#section-5.2.2.7).
8. **Background network failure.** A supporting private cache serves a stale copy within the modeled window and attempts revalidation, but the network fails. **Expected decision:** record the stale response and failed attempt separately; do not claim fresh code was installed or that this directive alone authorizes serving indefinitely. Evaluate subsequent requests and any independent `stale-if-error` permission under their own conditions. [RFC 5861 sections 3–4](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5861.html#section-3).
9. **No traffic in the window.** A response becomes stale at age 900 seconds; no request arrives until age 4,600 seconds, and no other validation happens. **Expected decision:** elapsed time alone did not trigger the request-driven asynchronous validation described in the RFC example. The first later request is beyond the modeled extension window, so normal handling or a separately applicable stale permission must be considered; do not assert the background refresh already ran. [RFC 5861 section 3.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5861.html#section-3.1).
10. **Changed code versus speed.** A security-sensitive bootstrap fix has just been published, and an operator proposes a one-hour stale allowance to reduce latency. **Expected decision:** flag that an eligible stale response could delay adoption of the fix for some requests. Weigh the acceptable exposure and rollback/update needs against latency, consider a narrower policy or other controlled release/invalidation measures, and verify actual cache behavior before use. Neither a short freshness lifetime nor an asynchronous attempt guarantees immediate rollout.
11. **Misread experimental labels and precision.** A report says the enabled 1% was experimental, the disabled 1% was control, and the 0.5% revenue increase applies to every publisher. **Expected decision:** correct the labels to the article's enabled control and disabled experimental groups, report the 0.5% as the case's observed revenue increase, and reject the universal claim. Ask for group-level denominators, measurement definitions, uncertainty and assignment details before drawing a more precise conclusion. [Google case](https://web.dev/case-studies/ads-case-study-stale-while-revalidate).
12. **Header as proof of outcomes.** A single response shows the three historical directive values, but no cache trace or experiment data. A dashboard claims 2% more early loads and a verified production improvement. **Expected decision:** reject the performance claim and any assertion of actual stale reuse or successful background validation. The header documents only that response's policy; collect scoped cache observations and an appropriately designed comparison before estimating outcomes. The historical GPT sample is not a measurement of this hypothetical site. [Google case](https://web.dev/case-studies/ads-case-study-stale-while-revalidate); [RFC 9111](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9111.html).
